# Dog Training and Behaviour

This guide covers friendly, reward-based training and how to read what a dog is telling you.

## Reward-based training

Modern trainers use positive reinforcement: reward the behaviour you want and the dog repeats it. Rewards can be small food treats, praise, a toy or a game. Give the reward within a second or two of the behaviour, so the dog links the two. Keep training sessions short, about five to ten minutes, and end on a success. Punishment and shouting tend to make dogs anxious and do not teach what you want instead.

## The basic commands

- **Sit.** Hold a treat just above the nose and move it back over the head. When the bottom lowers, say "sit" and reward.
- **Down.** From a sit, lower a treat to the floor between the paws until the dog lies down.
- **Stay.** Ask for a sit, say "stay", take one step back, return and reward. Slowly increase the distance and time.
- **Come (recall).** Say the dog's name and "come" in a happy voice and reward generously every time. Never call your dog to you to scold it.
- **Leave it and drop it.** These keep dogs safe around dropped food and things they should not chew.
- **Loose-lead walking.** Stop when the lead goes tight and move on when it is slack, so the dog learns that a loose lead means progress.

## Socialization

The most important window for socializing a puppy is roughly the first three to fourteen weeks of life. During this time introduce a puppy gently and positively to new people, other vaccinated dogs, different surfaces, car rides, handling and everyday noises. A well socialized puppy is more likely to grow into a confident, calm adult. Older dogs can still learn and improve with patient training.

## House training and crates

Take a puppy outside after sleeping, eating, playing and drinking, and reward it immediately for going in the right place. A crate can be a safe den if it is introduced gradually with treats and never used as punishment. Puppies cannot hold their bladder for long, so they need frequent trips outside.

## Reading dog body language

A wagging tail does not always mean a happy dog. A loose, wide wag with a relaxed body is friendly. A stiff, high, fast wag can signal arousal or tension. Other signals include:

- **Play bow:** front legs down and bottom up, an invitation to play.
- **Relaxed face and soft eyes:** a comfortable dog.
- **Yawning, lip licking, turning the head away:** signs of stress or unease.
- **Showing the whites of the eyes ("whale eye"), tucked tail, ears pinned back:** a fearful dog who needs space.
- **Growling:** a warning. Do not punish growling, because it is how a dog says it is uncomfortable. Instead, find out why and give the dog space.

## Common behaviour problems

- **Chewing:** puppies and bored dogs chew. Offer safe chew toys and enough exercise.
- **Barking:** work out the cause, whether it is boredom, alerting or loneliness, and teach a quiet signal with rewards.
- **Jumping up:** ignore it and reward four paws on the floor.
- **Pulling on the lead:** use the loose-lead method and consider a front-clip harness.
- **Separation anxiety:** build up time alone slowly and seek a trainer or vet if the dog is very distressed.

## Breed tendencies

Breed gives clues about what a dog enjoys, but every dog is an individual. Herding breeds such as the Border Collie may try to herd children. Terriers may dig. Hounds follow their noses and can be hard to recall. Retrievers love to carry things in their mouths. Northern breeds such as the Husky love to run and may escape if bored. Matching a breed's energy level to your lifestyle matters more than looks.

## When to get help

If a dog shows aggression or fear that you cannot manage, ask a vet to rule out pain or illness and then contact a qualified, force-free trainer or a veterinary behaviourist.
